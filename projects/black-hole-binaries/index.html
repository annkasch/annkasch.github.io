A stellar fossil record

By Ann-Kathrin Schuetz

Gravitational-wave paleontology asks us to read the lives of massive stars in what they left behind: black holes.

Paleontologists reconstruct dinosaurs from fossil bones; astronomers can reconstruct stellar history from black holes revealed by their mergers.

01 / Massive stars

The stars that made
much of our oxygen

Massive stars are rare—only a small fraction of stars are born with about ten times the Sun’s mass or more. Yet their influence reaches into our own bodies. Deep in their cores, fusion builds oxygen. Their winds and supernovae scatter that oxygen and other elements through galaxies, enriching the gas from which new stars and planets form. The hydrogen in water reaches back to the early Universe; its oxygen has a stellar history. Carl Sagan’s words, “We are made of star-stuff,” are more than a beautiful image: much of the oxygen in us was forged inside massive stars.

Those stars live for only a few to a few tens of millions of years. Some end in supernovae—among the Universe’s most powerful explosions—and leave neutron stars or black holes. When two massive stars form together, both may leave black holes. The black holes can then orbit for billions of years before finally merging.

Stellar background: NASA’s overview of stars ↗; Kroupa (2001), the distribution of stellar birth masses ↗; Kobayashi, Karakas & Lugaro (2020), the origins of the elements ↗.

02 / Reading the remnants

Studying massive stars
through black holes

By the time two black holes merge, their progenitor stars may have been gone for billions of years. The black holes’ masses and spins provide clues to those earlier stellar lives.

When black holes merge, their collision sends gravitational waves across space. Detectors on Earth capture those faint ripples; their signal lets us infer the black holes’ properties. Across many mergers, the clues can reveal the histories of stars that are long gone, including stars born in earlier cosmic epochs.

LIGO and Virgo: measuring spacetime

LIGO—the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory—has two detectors in the United States, at Hanford and Livingston, each with 4-km arms. Virgo, near Pisa in Italy, has 3-km arms.

These instruments split laser light between two perpendicular arms and recombine the returning beams. A passing gravitational wave produces tiny differences in the arms’ lengths, changing how the light interferes.

Comparing signals across the network helps distinguish an astrophysical event from local noise. Differences in arrival time and signal strength also constrain the source’s location on the sky.

These observations give us the properties of the remnants. Connecting them to the stars that formed them requires comparing entire populations with predictions from stellar-evolution models.

03 / Reconstructing a population

Reconstructing stellar history
with forward models

To read this fossil record, we evolve possible stellar populations forward and compare the mergers they predict with those we observe.

A forward model starts with a population of stars and a set of physical assumptions. It follows their evolution to predict the remnants, the binaries that survive, and the time each pair takes to merge. Combining those outcomes with the history of star formation gives a predicted merger population across cosmic time.

The comparison is statistical. Different initial masses, compositions, and interactions can produce similar black holes, so a single merger rarely identifies a unique stellar history. Across many events, a model must explain the black holes’ properties, how often mergers occur, and how that population changes with cosmic time. Accounting for measurement uncertainty and detector sensitivity lets us test which assumptions are consistent with the observed catalogue.

Reconstructing those histories takes more than one simulation. We have few direct observations of the progenitor stars, so models must cover many possible stellar and binary properties, physical assumptions, and birth environments—from one galaxy’s metal content to another’s. And in each simulated population, only a tiny fraction of binaries merge. The answer is hidden among rare outcomes: finding it with acceptable statistical uncertainty requires large populations, repeated across many possibilities. That is what makes the calculations so expensive.

Each model predicts a possible fossil record. The task is to find which predictions agree with the mergers we observe—and to make enough predictions to test the possibilities.

04 / The physics in COMPAS

From stellar evolution
to a cosmic merger rate

COMPAS evolves many pairs of massive stars, following both stars and their orbit to predict the remnants they leave behind.

It draws initial masses and orbits at chosen metallicities, then applies rapid prescriptions for stellar evolution, winds, mass transfer, envelope evolution, and core collapse. These track changing masses, radii, and orbits to determine which binaries survive and when their remnants merge.

Connecting the population to a cosmic merger rate

Each population gives a merger yield per unit stellar mass, η, and a distribution of birth-to-merger delays, pd. Weighting these by star formation at each birth time and metallicity gives:

R(t|θ)=∫dZ∫0tdτψ(Z,t−τ)η(Z,θ)pd(τ|Z,θ)

Here ψ is the stellar mass formed per volume, time, and metallicity at the birth epoch t − τ; η = Nmerge/M⋆ is the yield per total stellar mass represented; pd is the normalized delay density; and θ specifies the adopted stellar physics. R is the number of mergers per volume and source-frame time. Comparison with detections also accounts for detector sensitivity and cosmological time dilation.

05 / Making it faster

Exploring more possible
stellar histories

Together with my collaborators, I developed RESOLVE to predict the fraction of simulated binaries that produce merging black hole pairs, with uncertainty estimates.

In our study, a COMPAS run of one million binaries took approximately four CPU hours. A thousand such populations would require about 4,000 CPU hours. RESOLVE learns from many small populations and a few large ones, then predicts this formation efficiency at new parameters without evolving a fresh population each time:

ε(Z,θ)=1N∑i=1NXi

For N equally weighted simulated binaries, Xi = 1 if a system forms two black holes that merge within the age of the Universe, and 0 otherwise. This fraction ε becomes the mass-normalized yield η after accounting for the represented stellar mass.

We varied metallicity, common-envelope efficiency, and remnant-kick dispersions, and validated the emulator against 200 independent large populations. Extending it to the full cosmic population also requires predictions for merger properties and delay distributions.

Faster predictions let us test more possible stellar histories against the observed fossil record—and investigate which physical processes shaped the massive stars behind it.
